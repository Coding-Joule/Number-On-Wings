Hello

Twenty-one stones. Take one, two or three. Whoever takes the last one wins.

    Why it keeps winning

    Count what you leave, not what you take.

    Call a position lost if the player about to move loses against perfect play. Zero stones is lost: you have nothing to take, and your opponent has just taken the last one and won.

    Now build upwards from there. From a multiple of four you can take one, two or three, leaving 4k − 1, 4k − 2 or 4k − 3 — never a multiple of four. From anything that is not a multiple of four you can always take the remainder and leave one.

    So the lost positions are exactly the multiples of four. You can never move from one into another, and from anywhere else you can always move into one. That is the whole game.

    21 = 4 × 5 + 1, so take one, leave 20, and then mirror: whatever IrAcoNAl takes, you take 4 − that. It runs out of stones on its own move, every single time.

    If the most you could take were m rather than three, the invariant would be mod (m + 1) — the same argument with a different modulus. And this is the small end of a large theory: every impartial game of this kind carries a Sprague–Grundy value, and a position is lost exactly when that value is zero. Nim with several piles is this same idea, with XOR standing in for the remainder.
